D&D 5e Guides: The Math Behind Every Great Session

Five deep-dive guides · Worked examples in every one · Written by the person who built the tools

Most D&D advice tells you what to do; almost none of it shows you the math that makes it work. This library is the fix — five fully researched guides that walk through the numbers behind hit points, resistances, challenge rating, encounter difficulty and dice probability, every claim traced back to the official Dungeon Master's Guide and the SRD 5.1, every formula paired with the free tool that automates it.

Read them in order and they build on each other: HP first, then the traits that change it, then the rating that summarizes both, then whole-encounter difficulty, and finally the dice underneath everything. Each guide opens with a story from a real table, closes with a featured-snippet-ready FAQ section, and takes about ten minutes to read. No paywalls, no ads, no "join my Patreon to see the table."

Why These Five Guides?

Because they're the five questions that come up in every DM's prep, in this exact order: "How much HP does this thing have?" → "Why did it survive so long?" → "What CR should it be?" → "Is this fight fair?" → "Are my dice rigged?" Sites like Arcane Eye cover classes and spells brilliantly — this library covers the numerical backbone those guides assume you already know. Together they form a complete DM math toolkit, each paired with the matching free tool: the Hit Dice Calculator, Effective HP Calculator, CR Calculator, Encounter Builder, and Probability Dashboard.

How These Guides Were Built

Every formula here comes from the official rulebooks — the Player's Handbook (2014), Monster Manual (2014) and Dungeon Master's Guide (2014), cross-checked against the SRD 5.1 released under CC BY 4.0. Where the books give a shortcut (fixed average HP, the encounter multiplier), the guides show both the shortcut and the full derivation, so you can trust the number and understand it. Each article closes with its sources; nothing on this site is homebrew math wearing an official costume.

Start anywhere — or start at the calculator. Every guide stands alone with its own examples and FAQ. If you'd rather learn by doing, open the full 20-tool toolkit, paste a statblock's HP line, and come back here when you want the reasoning behind the numbers.
