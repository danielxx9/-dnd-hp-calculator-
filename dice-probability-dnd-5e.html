D&D 5e Dice Probability: The Odds Every DM Should Know

Your rogue needs a 12 on the d20 to pick the vault lock. What are the odds she makes it? If you said "about 50-50," you're close enough for storytelling — but the real answer (45%) is the difference between a lock that opens on turn one and a door that eats three sessions of progress. D&D 5e dice probability isn't math-class trivia: it's the skill that turns guessing into aiming, for DCs, boss survival, advantage, and every "should I take Great Weapon Fighter?" argument your table will ever have.

During one boss fight my dice threw three critical hits in four rounds, and the players inspected them for loaded weights. I sat down that night, ran the actual probability, and realized a 45% hit chance hitting 3 of 4 times is rare — but not one-in-a-million rare. Knowing the math made me a calmer DM and a kinder target of suspicion.

This guide gives you the handful of formulas that cover 95% of table situations — hit chance, advantage, multi-dice averages, expected rounds to kill — each with a worked example you can reuse verbatim. Hit Chance: The One Formula That Rules Combat

Every attack roll in 5e is "d20 + bonus vs. AC, hit if total meets AC." That compresses into a single sentence worth memorizing:

Hit chance = (21 − (AC − attack bonus)) ÷ 20

A fighter with +5 to hit against AC 15 needs a total of 15: she hits on 10 through 20, which is eleven faces of twenty — 55%. The same fighter against AC 18 hits on 13+ → 40%. Quick reference rows you'll use constantly:

Attack bonusvs AC 13vs AC 15vs AC 17vs AC 18
+355%45%35%30%
+565%55%45%40%
+880%70%60%55%

Two structural truths fall out of this table. First, every point of AC or attack bonus is worth exactly 5% — flat, linear, always. Second, in 5e's sweet spot both sides hover around 55–65%, which is why combat feels reliably dicey without ever feeling random-hostile. The designers tuned that on purpose.

Advantage: The Best-Documented Bonus in the Game

Advantage rolls two d20s and keeps the higher — probability-wise, you succeed if either die succeeds:

P(success with advantage) = 1 − (1 − p)², where p is your normal chance.

Worked numbers you'll want at arm's reach:

  • Normal 45% (need a 12) → advantage: 1 − 0.55² = 69.75%
  • Normal 55% → advantage: 1 − 0.45² = 79.75%
  • Normal 25% (a hard DC 15 with +1) → advantage: 43.75% — advantage nearly doubles bad odds.

Notice the pattern: advantage helps most when odds are worst, adding up to +25 percentage points at the bottom of the range and shrinking to +5 near the top. Disadvantage is the mirror image: 1 − p², and it hurts bad odds least and good odds most. And for the Trifecta: Elven Accuracy (triple roll, keep highest) runs 1 − (1 − p)³ — that 45% chance climbs to 83.4%. Sneak-attack assassins aren't lucky; they're compounding.

Crit Odds: The Numbers Behind the Legend

A natural 20 is always 5% — advantage can't make it likelier, it can only add a second 5% ticket: 9.75% (1 − 0.95²). Elven Accuracy pushes it to 14.26%. Flip side: the chance of at least one nat 20 in a 10-attack adventuring day is 1 − 0.95¹⁰ ≈ 40% — and over a whole campaign, "impossible" streaks are routine. That's the math my players should have quoted back at me.

Disadvantage: The Mirror in the Glass

Disadvantage keeps the lower die — P(success) = p² — and its cruelty is also asymmetric: the better your normal odds, the more disadvantage stings. A 55% attack drops to 30.25% (nearly halved), while a desperate 25% only falls to 6.25% (it was almost lost anyway). Strategically, this means disadvantage is a tax on reliable attackers, which is why darkness, Prone-ranged penalties and ogrish at-will effects feel so oppressive against martial parties — and why granting disadvantage to the boss's three attacks per round is often a bigger debuff than any cantrip. When you weigh a level-up or feat that imposes disadvantage, price it as roughly the mirror of advantage: up to −25 points of hit chance against good attackers, trivially small against bad ones.

Three Dice Facts That Feel Wrong (But Are Mathematically True)

Human intuition is terrible at randomness — these are the three illusions that cause most table superstitions, and the numbers that break them:

  • The gambler's fallacy. After five misses in a row, that 55% attack does not become "due" — each d20 forgets everything. The chance of six consecutive misses at 45% hit rate is 0.55⁶ ≈ 2.8% — rare enough to feel cursed, common enough to happen every few sessions somewhere at your table.
  • Clustering looks like rigging. In 100 random d20 rolls, runs of three identical results appear with near-certainty, and long "hot streaks" are statistically expected. My crit-streak story from the intro? Three crits in four attacks at 5% each is 1-in-2,000 for that exact window — but with thousands of attack rolls per campaign, you'll see it many times. The dice aren't rigged; humans are pattern engines.
  • More dice = more predictable, not more average. Rolling 8d6, individual rolls cluster tightly around 28 (standard deviation ≈ 4.8), while a single d20 is chaos. This is why big damage expressions feel "fair" and single-d20 death saves feel terrifying — variance, not averages, drives perceived swing.

Internalizing these three does more for your table's emotional health than any house rule: you'll stop hexing dice, your players will stop accusing them, and everyone can blame the ogre instead.

Multi-Dice Averages: 2d6 vs 1d12, Fireball Math, and Bell Curves

Averages are where most homebrew math quietly fails, so here are the anchors. A single die averages (faces + 1) ÷ 2: d6 → 3.5, d8 → 4.5, d10 → 5.5, d12 → 6.5, d20 → 10.5. Damage expressions follow the same pattern as monster HP: N × per-die average + flat bonus.

  • Great Weapon Fighting's classic debate — 1d12 vs 2d6: raw averages 6.5 vs 7. The d12 has a chance at 12; the 2d6 has a floor of 2 and clusters around 7. Over 100 swings, 2d6 wins by ~50 damage; over one swing, the d12's ceiling is real. Average favors 2d6; drama favors the d12.
  • Fireball's 8d6: 8 × 3.5 = 28 average, range 8–48. Against DC 15 with a +2 save, targets fail 60% of the time — expected damage per cast against a single target ≈ 28 × 0.6 + 14 × 0.4 = 22.4. That's the number to compare against a martial's output, not the fantasy 48.
  • Bell curves: 3d6 (used for rolling stats and common homebrew) peaks hard at 10–11 — the middle is twice as likely as the extremes. That's why "bell-curve" dice systems feel consistent and single-d20 systems feel swingy: the d20 has no curve at all, every face equally likely.

Expected Rounds to Kill: The DM's Most Useful Statistic

This is the calculation that makes boss HP design rational instead of vibes-based:

Expected rounds = monster HP ÷ (party DPR × hit chance)

Worked example — the classic 68 HP hill-giant-tier boss (that's 8d10+24, from our HP calculation guide) against a party of four dealing 30 raw DPR at a 55% hit rate: effective damage per round is 4 × 30 × 0.55 = 66, so the boss lasts 68 ÷ 66 ≈ one round. One round! If you want a four-round finale, the HP needs to be roughly 4 × 66 ≈ 264 — plus whatever effective HP mitigation you layer on.

Run the same numbers the other way for survival pressure: a CR-matched enemy dealing 30 DPR at 60% against your 45-HP wizard is expected to drop them in 45 ÷ 18 = 2.5 rounds of focused fire. That's why squishies hide behind tanks — and why "the monster beelines the wizard" is a difficulty dial, not a personality trait.

DC Benchmarks: Setting Numbers That Feel Fair

Difficulty classes are probabilities in disguise. The d20 baseline (competent adventurer, +5 bonus): DC 10 = 80%, DC 15 = 55%, DC 20 = 30%. Three table-tested rules fall out of this:

  • DC 15 is the "honest" default — meaningful risk for average builds, comfortable for specialists. If you can't justify a number, 15 is defensible everywhere.
  • Above DC 20, only specialists should ever succeed — that's by design. If the whole party should pass, the DC isn't 22; the task isn't a roll.
  • Let advantage do the flavor. Instead of DC 18 for the "clever approach," set DC 15 and grant advantage — same success math, better table feel, and players can see their cleverness working.

Frequently Asked Questions About 5e Dice Probability

What are the odds of rolling a natural 20 with advantage in 5e?

9.75%. Each d20 has a 5% chance, so with advantage the chance is 1 − 0.95² = 0.0975. With Elven Accuracy (three dice, keep the highest) it rises to 1 − 0.95³ ≈ 14.26%. Crits double on advantage but never compound beyond the dice count.

Is 2d6 better than 1d12 in D&D 5e?

On average, yes: 2d6 deals 7 per roll against the d12's 6.5, and never rolls below 2. The d12 answers with a 12 that 2d6 can never match. Over many attacks 2d6 wins; for a once-per-fight dramatic swing, the d12's ceiling is real damage potential.

What percentage is a DC 15 check in 5e?

It depends on the bonus, but the benchmarks are: +5 → 55%, +3 → 45%, +0 → 30%. With advantage on the +3 case it jumps to 69.75%. A DC 15 is deliberately the game's "moderate" anchor — risky for the untrained, routine for specialists.

How do you calculate how long a monster survives in 5e?

Divide the monster's effective HP by the party's expected damage per round — DPR times hit chance, summed across attackers. A 68 HP boss facing 66 expected damage per round survives about one round; quadruple the HP for a four-round fight. Simulation tools refine this estimate over thousands of rounds.

Conclusion: Stop Guessing, Start Aiming

Dice probability in D&D 5e comes down to five tools you now own: the hit-chance formula (5% per point), the advantage equation (1 − (1 − p)²), the multi-dice average (N × (faces + 1) ÷ 2 + bonus), expected rounds (HP ÷ expected damage), and DC anchors (15 honest, 20 specialist-only). Use them and the dice stop being weather and start being terrain — visible, mappable, something you design around instead of pray against.
